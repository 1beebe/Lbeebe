the roadmap

Four tracks, 90 days

Champions, the community of practice, and active discovery with business units are where people surface business problems. SAIL decides what's worth testing, and the portfolio tracks every initiative from intake to graduation, so leaders see one trusted picture.

I'd start from what's already running: SAIL, the AI Champions, and the Lunch & Learn. This plan builds on them instead of reinventing them. Being present in the Learning Community is how I stay close to the participants Year Up serves and understand their needs from a workforce readiness perspective.

  • Community of practice: the innovation flywheel. People try something, share it, and get others to try. It starts with the Lunch & Learn and the AI Champions who lead sessions. A shared knowledge base, an async channel like Slack, and an internal newsletter keep it going in between.
  • AI Champions: a national network of staff building with AI across every function, and the organization's eyes and ears. I would co-lead it.
  • SAIL: decides what's worth testing, using a product discovery loop. I also go to business units directly to find problems worth solving.
  • Portfolio: the single trusted view of every AI initiative, tracked from intake through graduation, not just the winners: status, cost, adoption, outcomes, and readiness to graduate. Each one is tied to the business problem it solves. The rubric for judging builds in the portfolio comes out of discovery: I'd work with the team to agree on the metrics first, then stand up the rubric.

Day 30, 60, and 90

This is my proposal. I'd adjust it after the first month of listening.

By day 30listen and map
  • Every AI initiative is inventoried in the portfolio, with its status, cost, adoption, and outcomes.
  • Listening conversations are done with SAIL, the AI Champions, and business units.
  • Time in the Learning Community, listening to participants and their needs.
By day 60co-design
  • The metrics for judging builds are agreed with the team, and the intake and graduation rubric is co-designed with SAIL on top of them.
  • The first pilots are running, each with a test card.
  • The first portfolio view is shared with leaders.
By day 90decide and share results
  • The first decisions are made against the rubric: graduate, not yet, or no.
  • The first before-and-after stories are shared.
  • A staff readiness plan is ready ahead of the early-2027 license rollout.

the culture underneath it all

All four tracks rest on a culture of building in public. When people share what they're trying while it's still unfinished, others pick it up and try it too, and that's what keeps the flywheel turning. The same habit takes care of communicating value, because the work and its results are already out where leaders, employers, and funders can see them.

the bar for "graduated"

Year Up United already has a model

The 2025 Impact Report describes a predictive internship matching tool. I think it's the best picture of what a graduated AI initiative should look like here, so I would use it to set the bar for "readiness to graduate" in the portfolio.

What makes it the model, and the bar I'd hold every pilot to:

  • It solves a real bottleneck. Matching used to take almost an hour of staff time per student.
  • It has a before and after. The time savings were measured in real use.
  • Staff keep the final call. The tool recommends, people decide, and staff satisfaction went up.
  • Equity is checked. Every match is reviewed against EEOC guidelines.
  • It helps Year Up United grow. Placements can scale without staffing costs growing at the same rate.
how this role goes wrong

Four ways to fail in this role

In our conversation, Eduvijes described how someone would fail in this role. Here is how I'd avoid each one.

way to fail #1

Can't co-create

  • What I do instead: I build with people. I listen first and write every pilot's test card with the team that will use it.
  • Proof: when eight international partners moved to one application portal, all eight adopted it and support requests dropped about 30%.
way to fail #2

Can't design

  • What I do instead: I design the test before anything is built: the problem, the person, and what success looks like, plus the metrics that tell us we're getting there.
  • Proof: in a Claude for Teams rollout I designed, staff confidence using AI rose from 70% to 92%.
way to fail #3

Can't share the value and impact

  • What I do instead: Because we defined success and the metrics up front, the impact story is mostly written before the pilot ends. Every win gets a before and after, told by the staff who did the work.
  • Proof: my AI learning community, the AI Architect Program, drew 144 registrants with no paid marketing.
way to fail #4

Can't handle ambiguity

  • What I do instead: I define success with others, then build clarity as we go. I document decisions and put just enough structure in place to meet ambiguity with clear eyes.
  • Proof: on a youth language program, we agreed on one measure together and learners meeting their goals went from 72% to 93%.
how I handle ambiguity

The product discovery loop

Inside the SAIL funnel, I run a product discovery loop borrowed from lean and agile practice. I wrap it in three commitments I make with the people who will live with the results:

  • Co-define success with SAIL and the teams served
  • Co-design the system, including the intake and the rubric
  • Build in public, so learning sticks and impact is visible
  1. Discover. Embed with the people closest to the problem and listen.
  2. Experiment. Run small tests side by side, then iterate.
  3. Measure. Score results against the rubric we agreed on.
  4. Discern. Decide what's worth building, and kill the rest early.
  5. Hand off. Give winners to a product team with a named owner.
